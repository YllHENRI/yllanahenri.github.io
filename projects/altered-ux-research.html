Overview

Altered® is a phygital trading card game, launched in September 2024, that links a physical card collection to a digital app.

As part of our UX Research coursework, my classmate and I chose to investigate how players actually manage their card collections across both formats, physical and digital, and how that connects to deck-building, a closely related activity.

This case study focuses on the research process: from hypotheses and interviews to synthesis and recommendations.

Role

UX Researcher (paired with equal responsibilities; interviews, recruitment, protocol design, data analysis, and recommendations were fully shared with my classmate)

Timeline

2 weeks

Context

Academic project for the UX Research course at École Supérieure du Digital (ESD)

Context & Hypothesis

Altered’s phygital model is still new, and its approach to helping players manage a collection across two formats hadn’t been closely studied. We wanted to understand whether this dual system created friction, confusion, or whether players found real value in it.

Hypothesis

We defined five hypotheses to guide our research, each paired with measurable indicators:

  • Hypothesis 1

    A mismatch between physical and digital cards may cause users to lose track of their own collection.

  • Hypothesis 2

    Finding a specific card, either in the physical or digital collection, may be difficult.

  • Hypothesis 3

    Users may be demotivated by the sheer number of cards to manage.

  • Hypothesis 4

    Altered’s proposed storage method may not actually help players manage their collection.

  • Hypothesis 5

    The phygital aspect may attract new players specifically because of its “modern” and “innovative” feel.

Recruitment

We recruited participants directly from Altered’s official Discord community. It was the fastest way to reach active, engaged players and make our call for participants clearly visible.

Of the 10 participants that answered our call, one was interviewed in person (our professor, an Altered player himself); the rest were remote, since participants were located across France and one in Quebec.

Who we talked to

All 10 participants were men aged 18-54, with a wide range of life contexts and play styles: students on a tight budget, fathers who played with their spouse and children, casual players, and dedicated collectors. Most were based in France; one was in Quebec.

Test format

Each session lasted between 40 minutes and 1h30 and followed a 3-step structure:

An introductory interview (5-10 min) covering tech comfort level and card-game experience, scenario-based user tests (30-40 min covering physical card management, digital card management, and deck building), and a closing interview with open-ended questions (it was supposed to last 10-20 min but it was at this moment that we often ended up engaging in very interesting conversations with the participants, diving deeper into their visions of the game and feedback).

Findings

Hypothesis check

After conducting all 10 interviews, we went back through our five initial hypotheses to see which held up against what players actually told us.

Interestingly, most of our assumptions about user frustration didn’t hold. Indeed, players turned out to be far more resourceful and at ease with managing a phygital collection than we expected. The two hypotheses that were confirmed pointed less to confusion, and more to Altered’s own tools not quite matching how players already organize their collections.

Below is what we found for each hypothesis, illustrated with real feedback from participants.

Hypothesis 1

Mismatch between physical and digital collections

Result: Not confirmed. Participants didn’t feel particularly disturbed by the phygital aspect of the game. When it came to gifting or trading cards, most relied on external tools like Excel or Google Sheets to track their collection rather than feeling lost.

“It’s great to have your collection in the app, but it still lacks some tracking.”

Hypothesis 2

Difficulty finding a specific card

Result: Not confirmed. Participants knew their collections very well and could easily locate cards, whether physical or digital.

“To find a physical card, I know my binder well, so I search by hand directly.”

Hypothesis 3

Demotivation from managing card quantity

Result: Not confirmed. Users weren’t demotivated at all. Some genuinely enjoyed the collecting aspect, while others simply lent or gave away cards they no longer needed.

“I have one binder for uniques and the rest in other binders. I’m planning to buy new binders for new sets, with one binder per set!”

Hypothesis 4

Altered’s storage method doesn’t help collection management

Result: Confirmed. Users don’t use Altered’s proposed storage solutions, finding them too expensive and poorly suited to their collection. They prefer external storage like boxes or binders that better match their needs.

“I bought a binder made by Altered, but I found it wasn’t suited… you can’t even store a full set because there isn’t enough room.”

Hypothesis 5

Phygital management attracts new players

Result: Confirmed. Users genuinely like the phygital concept, especially for how it simplifies deck building. The bulk physical-card scanning feature was particularly well received.

“I build my deck by hand first with my physical cards, and then scan them all at once to get my deck in the app!”

Cross-Analysis

Beyond testing our initial hypotheses, we wanted to understand why certain patterns emerged, specifically, whether a participant’s tech comfort, card-game experience, or collector/player mindset shaped how they interacted with their collection.

Crossing these variables against our findings surfaced some interesting nuances that a single-pass analysis would have missed.

Tech comfort vs. behavior

All participants rated themselves between 1-2 on a 7-point difficulty scale for everyday tech use, so we couldn’t meaningfully compare tech comfort levels between them. But notably, every participant, regardless of comfort level, reported something that frustrated them about the app.

Card game experience vs. deck building behavior

Players with little to no prior card-game experience were more likely to rely on external tools for deck building. Experienced card-game players were more forgiving of the app’s limitations: even when aware of its flaws, they were more accepting of the interface as given.

Experience vs. digital collection management

Regardless of experience level, 100% of participants found duplicate cards across different editions genuinely frustrating. More experienced players were additionally bothered by missing cards not being clearly flagged, and by cards owned in fewer than 3 copies not being clearly indicated.

Experience vs. physical collection management

Storage method (box vs. binder) didn’t correlate with experience. It was more a matter of personal preference. However, “collector” profiles tended to meticulously organize everything, while “player” profiles tended to set decks aside separately from the rest of their cards, even if still carefully sorted.

Recommendations

Drawing from our findings, we grouped our recommendations by collection type, focusing on the areas where Altered’s current tools fell short of how players actually manage their cards.

Physical collection: Storage

Problem: Altered’s proposed storage material isn’t used, as players find it poorly suited to their needs.

  • Run user tests to design a storage tool based on how players actually organize and use their cards.
  • Reconsider storage design around the cards’ own characteristics, rather than defaulting to conventional storage formats.

Digital collection: Search

Problem: Existing search functions don’t sufficiently help users find their cards.

  • Offer more filters for advanced search.
  • Optimize sorting methods to avoid bugs and inconsistent display.
  • Support advanced search by effect or keyword, not just card name.
  • Make search less rigid, offering suggestions based on what the user types rather than exact matches only.

Digital collection: Interface

Problem: The collection’s interface doesn’t support easy visualization.

  • Consolidate duplicate cards into a single grouped entry, rather than displaying each edition separately.
  • Let users choose if they wish to sort their cards by card name or by edition.
  • Reflect which cards are already owned directly within the display.

Digital collection: Deck building

Problem: Editing a decklist isn’t intuitive.

  • Build a more complete onboarding flow for deck building.
  • Let users choose to build a deck using only cards they currently own.
  • Surface commonly used cards more prominently.
  • Improve online deck-copying, with support for importing multiple list formats.

Digital collection: Trading & Gifting

Problem: The trade and gift function is unintuitive and impractical.

  • Let users track their trade and gift history (incoming/outgoing).
  • Consider a dedicated platform for trading and gifting.
  • Remove limits on the number of gifts a user can send.

Reflection & Conclusion

Our research led us to a nuanced picture of Altered’s collection-management experience: the app is “good enough” for now, but doesn’t yet offer the advanced options that the game’s full complexity calls for.

We identified a number of bugs, and the app can fairly be described as lacking optimization in places.

At the same time, we found a player base that is extremely invested. Invested enough that some players have already built their own external tools to better meet their needs, which is itself one of the clearest signals of where the product still falls short.

Learnings & Takeaways

This project was a genuine introduction to running a full research study end-to-end. From writing hypotheses and a test protocol, to recruiting real, currently active players, to synthesizing qualitative data into concrete recommendations.

Recruiting through Discord taught us how to move quickly to reach an engaged, relevant audience, even without a large existing network. And interviewing players across different countries pushed us to run consistent, well-structured remote sessions.

One of the more interesting lessons was watching our own assumptions get overturned: most of our hypotheses about user frustration didn’t hold up, which was a good reminder that research exists precisely to test assumptions, not confirm them.
